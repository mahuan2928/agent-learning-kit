RAG: A Beginner’s Guide

Get AI to answer questions by reading your company’s own documents. No background needed: first a metaphor that explains what RAG is, then a demo you can click through, so you can see for yourself how it finds information and where it goes wrong.

01 · Why you need it

The AI has never read your company’s rules

A model like Claude has read a huge amount of public material, but it has never read the internal rules of a particular restaurant company. Ask it “How much travel expense can I claim per day?” and it can only say it doesn’t know, or make up a number that sounds reasonable. The second is more dangerous.

There are two ways to make it able to answer:

Retrain the modelRAG: hand it the documents when you ask
How it works“Teach” the company documents into the modelEach time a question comes in, find the few relevant passages and give them to the model along with the question
When the rules changeRetrain the modelJust swap the documents; the very next question uses the new version
Can it cite a source?HardYes; you know exactly which passage the answer came from
Who can see whatHard to controlJust filter by permissions when searching

That’s why almost every company that wants “AI to answer internal questions” takes the second path. RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation: first retrieve (find the documents), then generate (write the answer).

02 · A metaphor

An open-book exam

The model

A very bright student who has never read this company’s rulebook.

RAG

During the exam, an assistant opens the rulebook to the relevant pages and lays them on the desk.

The answer

The student answers from those pages and notes “the answer is on page so-and-so.”

Note that the student hasn’t gotten any smarter and hasn’t “memorized” the rulebook. Every answer comes from whatever pages are open on the desk at that moment. So how good RAG is depends on one thing: did the assistant open the right pages? Open the wrong ones, and even the brightest student gets it wrong. This idea runs through the whole page.

03 · Three steps

Cut into cards, find the cards, answer from the cards

  1. 1
    Done once, in advance

    Cut the documents into small cards

    A rulebook is long. Handing the whole thing to the model is wasteful and makes it easy to miss things. So first, cut it by section into small cards, each about one topic. This step is called chunking.

    就業規則 → 「勤務時間」「休憩」「時間外労働と手当」「有給休暇」… (Work Rules → “Working hours,” “Breaks,” “Overtime and overtime pay,” “Paid leave”…)

  2. 2
    Every question

    Find the few cards most relevant to the question

    When a question comes in, pick the 3 to 5 most relevant cards out of all of them. This step is called retrieval. It’s the hardest part of RAG, and the one that matters most for quality.

    「交通費は1日いくらまで？」→ 経費精算規程 > 交通費 (“How much travel expense per day?” → Expense Rules > Travel expenses)

  3. 3
    Every question

    Give the cards and the question to the model

    Tell the model: “Answer only from these cards. If the cards don’t say, say you don’t know, and note which card each part came from.” This step is called generation.

    「交通費の上限は1日3,000円です。［経費精算規程（2026年版）> 交通費］」 (“The travel expense limit is 3,000 yen per day. [Expense Rules (2026 edition) > Travel expenses]”)

05 · Two ways to search

Match the words vs. match the meaning

Match the words: count how many words the question and a card have in common. The more they share, the more relevant the card. The demo uses the simplest version. The proper name is BM25; it works the same way, but gives more weight to rare words.

  • Good at: IDs, model numbers and proper nouns. For something like “E-221,” where the text must match exactly, nothing beats it.
  • Bad at: different wording. Ask about 残業代 (“overtime pay,” everyday word) when the rulebook says 時間外労働手当 (the formal term), and they share not a single word.

Match the meaning: first, a model turns each passage into a list of numbers, called a vector (or embedding). Think of it as pinning each passage to a spot on a “map of meaning”: passages that mean similar things land close together. When a question comes in, you pin it to the map too and pick the cards closest to it.

  • Good at: different wording and casual phrasing.
  • Bad at: IDs that differ by a single digit (E-221 and E-222 mean almost the same thing). Also, the scores all bunch together (in the demo, almost all are around 0.8), so only the ranking means anything, not the raw score.

Each has blind spots, so real systems usually use both together (called hybrid search) and add one more step that re-checks the ranking more carefully (called reranking). That’s what the next, hands-on page covers.

06 · Where it goes wrong

Badly cut cards

→ Hands-on 07 Declining to answer, 08 Verification

One more question runs through every chapter: when you change something, how do you know it got better and not worse? The answer is to prepare a set of questions, each labeled with the card that holds the right answer (called an evaluation set). After every change, run the whole set and check the hit rate. Every percentage on the hands-on page comes from this.

A beginner-level interview answer

“What is RAG?” The model has never read a company’s internal documents. So each time a question comes in, we first retrieve the most relevant passages from the document store and give them to the model along with the question, telling it to answer only from those passages and cite its sources. The benefits: document updates take effect immediately, answers come with sources, and access can be controlled by permissions. The hard part is retrieval: different wording finds nothing, old versions get found, content the user isn’t allowed to see gets found, and the model answers anyway when the documents have no answer. That’s why real systems add hybrid search, reranking, permission filtering and answer verification, and use an evaluation set to measure every change.
