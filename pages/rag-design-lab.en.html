Corpus & metadata: encode “who can see it” and “which version is current” in the data first

§2 Load / Metadata

Design-doc question: Which metadata fields do you need, and why decide them before chunking?

The 14 policy documents are fictional internal materials of 「サンプルダイニング」 (“Sample Dining”), deliberately seeded with four problems common in real-world knowledge bases:

  • Tiered access: the performance-review policy and the salary table are visible only to hr; the store manager handbook and manager meeting minutes only to manager / hq.
  • Superseded versions: the expense policy exists in a 2024 and a 2026 edition, with different travel-expense caps, filing deadlines, and receipt rules.
  • Near-duplicate entries: 15 POS error codes, several of which are all 「通信断」 (“connection lost”).
  • Long FAQ: a single section crams in 6 colloquial Q&A pairs — the easiest thing to chunk badly, and the most likely to hijack retrieval results.

The front matter at the top of each document is the metadata, and chunking copies it verbatim onto every chunk. Any filter you need at retrieval time (access, version) must exist at indexing time — adding it later means rebuilding the index.

Break it on purpose

In splitSection, delete the “keep Q: + A: lines together” condition and rerun E1. FAQ questions (the F series) start showing “retrieved the question line, but the answer is in the next chunk”. Chunk boundaries set the ceiling for retrieval; no reranker downstream can recover an answer that was cut apart.

How to say it in an interview

30-second version: I don't pick a chunking scheme by gut feeling. I label a small eval set first and compare a few strategies on recall@k. Empirically, the most robust approach is to split on headings, subdivide oversized sections along semantic units, and prefix the heading as context — but you still have to measure on your own data. In my experiments, fixed 200-character chunks split table headers from their rows and caused misses on table questions; overlap mitigates that, structure-aware chunking fixes it.

How do you choose chunk size?
Two constraints: how long an answer typically is (here a clause runs 50–150 characters), and how many chunks you can fit into generation (top 5 × 300 characters is trivial for the model). Too large and retrieval gets imprecise and citations get vague; too small and you split a sentence's condition from its conclusion.
Does contextual chunking help?
On this corpus it showed no gain: after prefixing the indexed text with 「文書名 > 見出し」 (doc title > heading), R@1 stayed at 76% and MRR went 0.87 → 0.86. The reason is that the body text of these policies already repeats the keywords in their headings. Its value shows up on documents whose body never names the topic (tables, or clauses like 「前項の場合は…」, “in the case of the preceding paragraph…”). I keep it mainly because citations need to display the source heading; whether to include it in the indexed text should likewise be decided by the eval set.

§4.1 Hybrid retrieval

Design-doc question: What are vector search, BM25, and hybrid each good at? Is hybrid always better?

Five retrieval methods compared on the section+ctx chunking. The last three columns are R@3 broken down by question type:

Retrieval methodR@1R@5MRRIdentifier R@3Paraphrase R@3Table R@3
BM25 (character bigrams)53%87%0.6888%70%67%
BM25 (hiragana bigrams removed)61%87%0.75100%75%100%

Hybrid RRF (raw BM25)71%89%0.81100%80%100%
Hybrid RRF (BM25, hiragana removed)76%95%0.86100%85%100%

This table contains two counterintuitive results, both of which you will hit in real knowledge bases:

  1. Hybrid retrieval did not beat pure vector search. Vector R@5 is 97%; hybrid is 95%. RRF is an equal-weight fusion: when BM25 ranks things wrong, it drags down results that the vector retriever ranked correctly. On a small 50-chunk corpus a multilingual embedding is already good enough, so BM25's upside (exact identifier matches) never materializes, while its noise does.
  2. Japanese tokenization decides whether BM25 helps or hurts. Character bigrams treat 「どうやって」 and 「されるの」 as terms too, so a colloquial question (「残業代ってどうやって計算されるの？」, “how is overtime pay calculated?”) ends up matching equally colloquial FAQ lines. Do one thing — drop pure-hiragana bigrams — and hybrid R@5 rises from 89% to 95%. The proper fix is morphological analysis with Sudachi / MeCab, keeping only content words.

So why use hybrid at all? Because as the corpus grows, vector search tends to fail on queries that need a literal match: identifiers, model numbers, names, clause numbers (E-221 and E-212 differ by one digit, and both are described as 「通信断」, “connection lost”). BM25 is the safety net there. This lab's corpus is too small to demonstrate that, so the next thing to look at is reranking (Chapter 04), which keeps the strengths of both retrievers.

§4.1 Rerank

Design-doc question: Why rerank after retrieval? What does it cost?

Retrieval (bi-encoder) encodes the query and the document separately and compares similarity: fast but coarse. Reranking (cross-encoder) feeds the query and document in together and scores each pair: slow but precise. The standard recipe is to retrieve 20 and keep the top 5 after reranking.

For the retrieval k, look at the R@k curve: once R@20 is close to 100%, that's enough. For the generation k, weigh context cost against distraction: the more chunks, the more likely the model is led astray by irrelevant content. Here R@3 is already 100%; taking 5 leaves headroom.
Chapter 05

Access filtering: filter at retrieval time — never rely on the model to keep quiet

This chapter answers

§4.1 Access filtering

Design-doc question: Where does access filtering go? Filter before or after ranking?

With no filtering at all, 11/46 questions had chunks the asker is not authorized to see in their top 5 results. For example:

  • K1 → D07#2
  • P1 → D09#2
  • P5 → D10#3
  • P11 → D09#1
  • T1 → D09#2, D09#1
  • T2 → D09#2, D09#1

Once those chunks are in the generation context, all you can do is hope the model “doesn't say it”. That isn't access control; it's prayer. So filtering has to happen at retrieval time: unauthorized chunks never enter the candidate set.

On this small corpus the gap is minor, but in an index of hundreds of thousands of chunks, when a regular employee asks a pay-related question, the top 5 could all be HR documents, and post-filter returns nothing at all. Chapter 06

Document versions: the old policy outranked the new one — every single time

This chapter answers

§4.1 Freshness filtering

Design-doc question: When a policy is updated, should the old version be removed from the index? How do you make retrieval return only the current version?

Three expense questions (travel-expense cap, filing deadline, receipts). Without any handling, the old version ranked above the new one on all 3:

  • S1 top 3: D02#1 D03#1 D02#2
  • S2 top 3: D02#2 D03#2 D02#1
  • S3 top 3: D02#3 D03#3 D14#1

The reason is simple: the two editions are worded almost identically, and the old one's phrasing (「原本を郵送」 “mail the original”, 「翌月5日」 “the 5th of the following month”) is even closer to the colloquial questions. Similarity cannot tell you which version is in force; only metadata can.

This lab uses two safeguards: at retrieval time, drop documents whose superseded_by is non-empty (in E5, stale-version hits fall to 0); at generation time, every retrieved result's title carries its update date, and the instructions state “when sources conflict, follow the one with the later update date”, guarding against missing metadata.

The old version itself is not deleted: audits need to answer “what was the rule at the time?”. The right approach is to keep it in the index but exclude it from retrieval by default, and include it explicitly via a parameter when querying history.

Why not have an LLM do the grading?
Reranker scores are free (local model), deterministic, and reproducible, and the threshold can be set from data. LLM grading costs an extra call and its scores drift. Only when the score distributions genuinely don't separate is it worth adding an LLM judgment within the ambiguous band.

How to say it in an interview

30-second version: I model RAG as a state graph: route, rewrite, retrieve, grade, generate, verify, fallback, with feedback loops at grading and verification — matching CRAG's “if retrieval is poor, correct the retrieval; if generation is untrustworthy, correct the generation”. Nodes that code can decide don't call a model; retrieval and grading run entirely locally. Generation uses the API's native citations, so sources can't be fabricated, and verification then checks whether each citation actually supports its sentence. Every loop has a retry cap.

Why write your own graph runner instead of using LangGraph?
So every line is visible while learning. The API is named after LangGraph (addNode / addEdge / addConditionalEdges / invoke), so porting is one-to-one. In production, LangGraph gives you checkpointers, interrupts (human-in-the-loop), and LangSmith tracing out of the box.
Can rewrite break the question?
Yes. That's why retrieval searches with both the original question and the rewritten queries, merges the results, reranks, and takes the highest reranker score. Rewriting can only add candidates; it can never make something the original question would have found disappear.
What about multi-turn conversations?
Pass the conversation history into the rewrite node and resolve coreferences (“what about that one?” → 「交通費の上限は？」 “what's the travel-expense cap?”); persist state with a checkpointer keyed by thread_id. In this lab every question is independent, so that layer isn't implemented.

The judge here does one very narrow thing: decide whether an answer contains a given key point. That's far more stable than scoring on a 1–10 scale.
